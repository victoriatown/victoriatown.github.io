‘AI Slop’ distorting our reality, the world is sleepwalking into disaster by Nesrine Malik Response

The Guardian article ‘‘AI Slop’ distorting our reality, the world is sleepwalking into disaster’, by Nesrine Malik, explores how the rise of what it calls “AI slop”, reflects social media platforms’ preference for fast, low-cost, endlessly generated content over human-made work. It argues that when content becomes cheaper and easier to produce through automation, quality and intention often become secondary to engagement. One idea that stood out to me was the suggestion that constant exposure to AI-generated images creates a kind of visual numbness and confusion, where everything feels simultaneously hyper-real and artificial. Reading this made me think about the use of AI in the fashion industry, which I have recently studied, particularly the controversy surrounding the use of AI-generated models in Vogue. The article’s concerns about authenticity, labour and perception feel extremely relevant when applied to this example of in the fashion industry, an industry so reliant on visual culture and human creativity.

The article suggests that tech companies believe that “content is content”, and that the less human labour involved, the better. This logic is clearly visible in the Guess advertisement featured in Vogue, where the Paris-based creative house Seraphinne Vallora used generative algorithms trained on runway imagery and campaign shots to create a hyper-realistic AI model. The campaign reportedly required no casting director, no green room, and no retoucher. What once demanded weeks of collaborative creative labour was compressed into an algorithmic process. From an efficiency standpoint, this mirrors the Malik’s argument that AI reduces cost and time, streamlining production in ways that appeal to corporations.

However, the backlash from Vogue readers reveals that fashion is not simply “content.” Fashion has always been deeply tied to identity, embodiment, and cultural storytelling. When subscribers cancelled their subscriptions and questioned the magazine’s decision, it demonstrated discomfort not only with the technology itself, but with what it symbolises, the displacement of human artistry and effort. Additionally, Malik notes that constant exposure to AI imagery alters how we perceive visual information , stating that “everything begins to land in a different way.” In the fashion industry, where beauty standards are primarily constructed by imagery, this shift is particularly significant.

Furthermore, the AI Guess model exemplifies the Malik’s concern about generative AI being trained on biased and pre-existing datasets. Malik mentions that research shows such systems often reinforce narrow ideals around ethnicity, gender and body type. The AI model in Vogue was described as “flawless”, and was an idealised blonde figure conforming to traditional beauty standards. This raises troubling questions as real models have fought for decades for diversity and representation in fashion publications. If AI systems are trained on historically biased imagery, they risk re-concentrating those norms rather than expanding representation, meaning that instead of progress, we may see regression disguised as innovation.

Moreover, Malik also describes the “desensitisation and overwhelming of visual palates” caused by AI saturation. In fashion, where imagery is already highly curated and retouched, the addition of AI could intensify this. If audiences are continuously exposed to fake perfection, the line between human and artificial becomes blurred. This produces the same “profound disorientation” that an article describes, that ‘if an AI-generated model is indistinguishable from a real one, what does authenticity mean? What happens to the emotional resonance of a photograph when its subject has no lived history, no body, no story?’

Ultimately, the Vogue controversy demonstrates that while AI offers efficiency and novelty, I believe that fashion cannot be reduced to algorithmic output without consequences. The Guardian’s critique of “AI slop” warns that when human labour and nuance are stripped away, something intangible is lost. In an industry built on storytelling, identity and cultural expression, the erosion of human work feels very concerning.
